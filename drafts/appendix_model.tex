\section{Mediator Model Details}
\label{app:training}

\subsection{Block Structure and Training}
\label{app:block_training}

\paragraph{Factorization.}
For each dataset the mediators are partitioned into the blocks of
Table~\ref{tab:roles}, and the mediator model is the product of one factor
per block~\eqref{eq:block_fact}. Table~\ref{tab:factors} lists the factors.
Every discrete mediator has its own categorical head $g_\phi$, and the
continuous mediators, which always form a single block placed last, share
one conditional flow $f_\theta$. Each factor conditions on $x$, on $z$, and on
\emph{all} mediators in earlier blocks, not only on the block immediately
before it. In German-Synth this is a superset of the true parents (for
example, savings depends on education only through job in the SCM), so the
true mechanisms remain within the model class. No factor conditions on a
mediator in its own block or in a later one. In the datasets used here every
discrete block contains a single mediator, so no within-block factorization of
categorical mediators is needed.

\paragraph{Discrete heads.}
Each head is a two-layer ReLU network with 64 units per layer, followed by a
linear layer that outputs one logit per category. Its input is the
concatenation of $x$, $\tilde z$, and the embeddings of its parent mediators.
The heads share one embedding table per discrete mediator, so a mediator has
the same embedding in every head that conditions on it. Categorical mediators are modelled as
unordered classes, including those that are ordinal, such as education and
hours bins. Ordinality enters only through the posterior-predictive gates
below.

\paragraph{Continuous block.}
Before training, each continuous mediator is mapped to a standard normal
marginal by a quantile transform (1{,}000 quantiles) fitted on the training
split. In ACS, hours and weeks are clipped to $[1,60]$ and $[1,52]$ first.
The flow is a stack of masked autoregressive rational-quadratic spline
transforms~\citep{durkan2019neural}, with a random permutation between
successive transforms and a standard normal base distribution
(architecture in Table~\ref{tab:flow_hparams}). Its context vector
$h=[e(w_{\mathrm{disc}});x;\tilde z]$ is passed to every conditioner, and the
flow has its own embedding tables for the discrete mediators. The
autoregressive order inside the flow is a density parameterization with no
causal meaning (Section~\ref{sec:flows}).

\paragraph{Training objective.}
All heads and the flow are trained jointly by minimizing the negative
log-likelihood~\eqref{eq:flow_training} on the training split. This is the sum
of one cross-entropy term per discrete head and the flow's negative
log-density. Each head is trained on the \emph{observed} values of its parent
mediators (teacher forcing), and the flow on the observed discrete mediators.
Apart from the shared embeddings of the heads, the factors have no
parameters in common, and the heads and the flow share none. The flow's log-density is measured in the quantile-transformed space, so reported
validation NLLs are not in natural units and are not comparable across
datasets.

\paragraph{Optimization.}
We use Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and mini-batches of 256 rows,
reshuffled every epoch. The gradient norm over all parameters is clipped at 1.
After each epoch we compute the validation NLL and halve the learning rate
after 5 epochs without improvement in it. Where posterior-predictive gates are
configured, we also compute the gate discrepancies on a fixed subset of
validation contexts (see \emph{Checkpoint selection} below).
We keep the eligible checkpoint with the lowest selection score. Training
stops after the configured number of epochs without improvement in that
score, counted from the first eligible checkpoint, or at the epoch cap
(Table~\ref{tab:flow_hparams}). Law School has no gates, so every epoch is
eligible and its selection score is the validation NLL. The seed fixes the
PyTorch and NumPy random states, so it fixes initialization, batch order, and
the posterior-predictive draws. Models are trained on a GPU when one is
available.

\paragraph{Sampling and interventions.}
Observational samples are drawn ancestrally in the order of
Table~\ref{tab:factors}. Each head samples from its softmax given the
already-sampled parents, and the flow transforms a fresh standard normal draw
given the sampled discrete mediators. Continuous samples are then mapped back
to natural units with the inverse quantile transform. Interventional samples
from $Q_a$ use the same factors (Section~\ref{sec:intervention}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item directly acted-on mediators are clamped to their action values;
  \item mediators in blocks before the earliest acted block keep their factual
        values;
  \item mediators in later blocks are redrawn from their factors, conditioned
        on the clamped and redrawn values;
  \item unacted mediators in an acted block keep their factual values.
\end{itemize}
Because Law School has a single block, every plan clamps or keeps each
mediator, and $Q_a$ is a point mass.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{app:implementation}

\paragraph{Software.}
The mediator model is implemented in PyTorch~\citep{paszke2019pytorch}. The
continuous block uses the conditional
\texttt{MaskedPiecewiseRationalQuadraticAutoregressiveTransform} from
\texttt{nflows} 0.14~\citep{nflows}, with linear tails, residual MADE
conditioners, random permutations between layers (when the block has more
than one coordinate), and a standard normal base distribution. The
conditioning vector is the concatenation of the discrete-mediator embeddings,
$x$, and the standardized $z$. Following the stated graph, German-Synth drops
$x$ from it (Section~\ref{sec:flows}). Continuous mediators are passed through
a scikit-learn \texttt{QuantileTransformer} with a normal output distribution,
fitted on the training split. The flow is trained in that space, and samples
are inverse-transformed to natural units before they are scored. Classifiers,
quantile transforms, and the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance
(\texttt{scipy.stats.wasserstein\_distance}) use scikit-learn and SciPy.

\paragraph{Numerical safeguard.}
In float32, the analytic inverse of a rational-quadratic spline can very
rarely produce a slightly negative discriminant, which \texttt{nflows}
rejects. When this happens inside a Monte Carlo batch, we split the batch
recursively and redraw only the affected rows, allowing at most eight redraws
per row before raising an error. No run reached that limit.

\paragraph{Checkpoint selection.}
After every epoch we evaluate the posterior-predictive discrepancies of
Section~\ref{sec:exp_design} on a fixed random subset of validation contexts.
For each context the full mediator vector is drawn ancestrally with the
context's own $(x,z)$. Discrepancies are computed overall and, for groups of
$X$ with at least the stated minimum number of validation rows, within each
group:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item total variation between generated and observed marginals of each
        categorical mediator;
  \item for mediators declared ordinal, $W_1$ between category codes divided
        by the number of categories minus one;
  \item for each continuous mediator, $W_1$ approximated on 101 quantiles,
        divided by the observed standard deviation (in the quantile-transformed
        space);
  \item the largest absolute difference between generated and observed
        pairwise correlations of the continuous mediators.
\end{itemize}
Each gated quantity is the maximum over mediators (and groups). A checkpoint
is eligible if all gated quantities are at or below their thresholds. Its
selection score is the validation negative log-likelihood plus the
score weight times the sum of the gated quantities. The selected checkpoint
minimizes this score among eligible checkpoints. The patience counter for
early stopping starts only once an eligible checkpoint exists, and training
fails if none is ever eligible. Joint total variation over all categorical
mediators is logged but not gated, since sparse joint tables have large
finite-sample variation.

\paragraph{Classifiers and effects.}
Table~\ref{tab:app_effects} reports validation performance and the
classifier-scale effect decomposition for every dataset and classifier. The
addressable share $|\NIE|/|\mathrm{TE}|$ separates the datasets: it is
0.85--0.90 on German-Synth and 0.69--0.79 on Law School, but only 0.13--0.24
on ACS and 0.14--0.17 on Adult, where most of the effect runs through the
direct path that mediator-based recourse cannot reach.

\paragraph{Full recourse metrics.}
Table~\ref{tab:app_recourse} extends Table~\ref{tab:main_results} with the
mean post-recourse disparity, the overshoot rate and size, and the standard
deviations of abstention and cost. Table~\ref{tab:app_compat} gives the
compatibility diagnostics behind the abstention causes, and
Table~\ref{tab:app_coverage} shows how many recipients our method would serve
at other tolerances $\varepsilon$. On ACS a looser $\varepsilon=0.15$ would
serve about half of the recipients rather than a quarter. On Adult no
tolerance below $0.15$ serves anyone, which matches the median
$\hat\varepsilon_\gamma$ of $0.12$--$0.15$.
